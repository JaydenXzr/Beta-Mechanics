01 / The American icon

Ford
Mustang.

A long hood, a short rear deck, and a name that became a whole category of car.

The Mustang arrived in 1964 with a simple but potent idea: make a sporty car feel exciting and attainable, then let its owner decide what kind of personality it should have. Its proportions did a lot of the talking. A long bonnet suggested power; a compact cabin and tight rear deck kept the silhouette athletic. The recipe was instantly recognizable, and the name soon became shorthand for the American pony car.

More than six decades later, the Mustang remains a shape-shifter. It can be a fastback built for a long highway, a convertible for warm evenings, a V8-powered coupe with a soundtrack of its own, or a track-oriented model with more cooling, grip, and chassis focus. The exact equipment changes with the year and trim, but the central promise stays familiar: a front-engine sports coupe that feels bold, expressive, and connected to the driver.

That versatility is a big part of its staying power. Owners personalize Mustangs, take them to weekend meets, commute in them, race them, restore them, and pass them between generations. Few cars carry such a large visual vocabulary: stripes, scoops, fastback rooflines, galloping-horse badges, and a seemingly endless variety of wheel and paint combinations. Even when parked, the Mustang looks as if it has somewhere to be.

Its history is not one unbroken specification or one single driving experience. Engines, platforms, safety equipment, and performance priorities have changed many times. Instead, the Mustang story is about evolution around a recognizable character. The modern car has to meet contemporary expectations for comfort, technology, and safety, while still feeling like a Mustang from the moment you see its stance and profile.

In a dream garage, the Mustang plays the role of the charismatic all-rounder. It has cultural weight without needing to be rare, and it can be enjoyed in a way that feels immediate rather than ceremonial. It is a car for people who want the drive itself to be part of the event: choose a road, pick a gear, let the engine work, and give the evening a soundtrack.

01 / DESIGN

Proportions that talk

The classic long-hood, short-deck layout gives the Mustang a forward-leaning stance. Designers have revisited the details over the decades, but its silhouette remains legible: muscular shoulders, a planted rear, and a cabin pushed toward the back of the car.

02 / PERSONALITY

One badge, many builds

Model years and trims matter. A daily-friendly version, a V8 GT, and a focused track model can have very different equipment and behavior. That range lets drivers choose the Mustang experience that fits them instead of forcing one definition of performance.

03 / CULTURE

A car people make their own

Paint, wheels, stripes, exhaust, restoration, racing—Mustang culture is an invitation to personalize. The car's story doesn't stop at the factory; each owner adds another chapter to a nameplate with a huge community behind it.

Why the Mustang still feels like a Mustang

Continuity is difficult when a car survives across generations. Regulations change, technology moves forward, and customers expect more refinement. The Mustang's answer has been to preserve its emotional cues while modernizing the hardware around them. The front-engine layout, assertive proportions, rear-drive heritage, and broad performance range create a link between eras, even when the actual cars are mechanically distinct.

That balance also explains why the car can be interpreted in so many ways. Some people love the sound and feel of a V8; others prize the styling, the open-air option, or the chance to build a personal project. Fans may disagree about the best generation, engine, or trim, but the debate is part of the appeal. The Mustang is not a museum object. It is an ongoing conversation about what a fun, expressive performance car should be.

Specifications depend on model year and market, so always check the exact car being discussed. The story that stays constant is simpler: the Mustang makes performance feel visible, audible, and approachable.

02 / The French exclusive

Bugatti
Centodieci.

A ten-car salute to a chapter of Bugatti history, reimagined with modern hypercar drama.

Centodieci means “one hundred and ten” in Italian, and the name points to the EB110: the ambitious supercar that marked an earlier, dramatic chapter in Bugatti's story. The modern Centodieci is not a simple replica. It borrows visual cues from the EB110 and translates them into a body designed around the Chiron-era platform and the scale of a contemporary hypercar.

The design is intentionally different from the smooth, horseshoe-grille-led look associated with the Chiron. Five small air openings sit beside the familiar central grille, the headlights are narrow, and the side profile uses strong geometric surfaces. At the rear, a complex arrangement of vents, lights, and diffuser elements makes the car look almost architectural. The result is a visual bridge: recognizable as a Bugatti, but with a much sharper and more graphic identity.

Underneath the bodywork is an 8.0-litre quad-turbocharged W16, specified for the Centodieci at 1,600 PS. That number is enormous, but the more interesting story is how the car packages it. Cooling, intake flow, chassis control, and aerodynamic stability have to work together. A hypercar cannot rely on power alone; it must manage heat, grip, airflow, braking, and the driver's confidence as one integrated system.

Exclusivity is part of the Centodieci's purpose. Bugatti planned a run of just ten cars, making the model a rare collector's piece from the outset. Its value is not only in its scarcity, but in the historical reference it carries. The car interprets the EB110 for a new era, showing how a modern manufacturer can acknowledge its past without simply copying it.

In a garage, the Centodieci feels like the centerpiece you approach slowly. Its story has several layers: a name that rewards translation, design that invites comparison, and engineering that belongs to the most extreme road cars of its period. The Bugatti experience is less about being loud in every moment and more about the sheer completeness of the object—its finish, its presence, and the knowledge that only a handful exist.

01 / HERITAGE

A name with a number

“Centodieci” translates to 110, a direct nod to the EB110. That connection gives the project its premise: celebrate an earlier landmark while expressing it through current design and engineering rather than reproducing the old car.

02 / FORM

Geometry over nostalgia

The EB110 reference is visible in the graphic details and the car's more angular character. Five side intakes, fine lighting elements, and sharply drawn surfaces distinguish it from the softer shapes of its platform sibling.

03 / RARITY

Ten individual commissions

A production run limited to ten makes every example an unusually personal object. For collectors, the Centodieci combines a clear historical reference with the rarity of a small-series, modern Bugatti.

Homage without imitation

The strongest tributes do more than repeat old shapes. They understand what made the original significant, then ask what that idea would look like now. The Centodieci uses the EB110 as a point of departure: a connection to Bugatti's return to the supercar world, and a reminder that the brand has repeatedly used engineering ambition to define itself.

Its styling is a deliberate counterpoint to the flowing curves of the Chiron. The tighter openings and hard-edged elements make the body appear almost hand-drawn from a different design language. Yet the car still has to perform the practical work of a modern hypercar: channel air, feed radiators, control temperature, and maintain stability. Form and function are not competing goals here; each surface has to justify itself.

Published figures describe the Centodieci's engine output and limited production, but individual specifications and market details can vary. The lasting impression is its combination of historical context, technical excess, and extreme scarcity.

03 / The Swedish engineer

Koenigsegg
Jesko.

A high-revving twin-turbo V8, a nine-speed transmission, and aerodynamics that put the air to work.

The Jesko is Koenigsegg's answer to a difficult question: how far can a road-legal hypercar push its engineering while remaining a coherent machine? Named after Jesko von Koenigsegg, father of company founder Christian von Koenigsegg, the car builds around a five-litre twin-turbo V8 and the brand's nine-speed Light Speed Transmission. The idea is not just to make a headline number. It is to make the power usable, the shifts immediate, and the car responsive across a broad range of driving.

Koenigsegg publishes 1,280 horsepower on standard gasoline and up to 1,600 horsepower on E85 biofuel where available. Those are manufacturer figures, and fuel availability matters. The unusual transmission is a major part of the story: nine forward gears in a compact unit designed to select a suitable ratio quickly, rather than simply following the familiar pattern of a conventional manual or dual-clutch gearbox.

The Jesko family includes two distinct aerodynamic interpretations. The Jesko Attack emphasizes high downforce and track use, with large external aero elements. The Jesko Absolut has a lower-drag body intended for high-speed stability. They share a foundation, but their priorities differ; the shape of the rear, the aerodynamic hardware, and the setup reflect what each version is designed to do.

That split is a reminder that speed is not one-dimensional. A circuit car needs to turn air into grip and confidence through corners. A low-drag machine has a different challenge: reduce resistance while keeping the car stable and manageable. The Jesko shows those choices openly. Its wings, vents, and surfaces are not hidden details; they are central parts of the design language.

Inside, the focus remains on the person driving. Compact displays and touch-sensitive controls bring functions close to hand, while a small instrument cluster sits directly behind the steering wheel. The details can feel almost playful alongside the serious engineering: a mechanical G-force meter, carefully arranged controls, and a cabin that treats information as part of the experience rather than an afterthought.

For enthusiasts, the Jesko is compelling because it feels like a collection of purposeful ideas. The V8 is only one piece. The transmission, the fuel strategy, the aero split between Attack and Absolut, and the driver's interface all answer different parts of the same problem. It is a hypercar that wears its engineering on the outside and invites you to keep asking how each part works.

01 / POWER

A V8 with a fuel-dependent headline

Koenigsegg lists 1,280 hp on standard gasoline and up to 1,600 hp on E85. That distinction matters: the maximum figure depends on the fuel, so it should not be read as a universal output in every market or driving condition.

02 / TRANSMISSION

Light Speed Transmission

The nine-speed LST uses a multi-clutch design to make fast gear selection across the ratios. Its compact packaging and shift strategy are part of the Jesko's technical identity, not just a number on a spec sheet.

03 / AERODYNAMICS

Attack or Absolut?

Attack focuses on downforce for circuit work; Absolut is designed around lower drag and high-speed performance. They share a name and core architecture, but their aero priorities create two different personalities.

Engineering the whole experience

On paper, the Jesko's headline output is astonishing. In the real design, however, power creates more questions than it answers. How does the engine breathe? How can the transmission choose a ratio without interrupting the acceleration? How does the body generate useful aerodynamic load without making the car unstable? And how can a driver understand what the machine is doing while everything happens quickly?

Koenigsegg's response is to treat those questions as a system. The twin-turbo V8, the Light Speed Transmission, the two aerodynamic configurations, and the cockpit controls are different pieces of one performance brief. The Attack can use substantial aerodynamic hardware to focus on track performance. The Absolut reduces drag for its own high-speed target. Neither approach is universally “better”; each is a different answer to what the car should prioritize.

Koenigsegg lists production of 125 Jesko Attack and Jesko Absolut cars. Published specifications are manufacturer claims and may depend on fuel, configuration, and market. The car's defining quality is the unusual depth of thought behind the numbers.
